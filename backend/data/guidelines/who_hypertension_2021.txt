== 1. WHEN TO START PHARMACOLOGICAL TREATMENT ==

WHO strongly recommends starting antihypertensive drug treatment for a confirmed
diagnosis of hypertension with systolic BP >=140 mmHg or diastolic BP >=90 mmHg
(moderate-to-high certainty evidence). For people with existing cardiovascular
disease, treatment should start at a lower threshold — systolic BP 130-139 mmHg
(strong recommendation). The same lower threshold is suggested for people without
known CVD but with high cardiovascular risk, diabetes mellitus, or chronic kidney
disease. Treatment should begin within 4 weeks of diagnosis; if BP is very high
(systolic >=160 or diastolic >=100 mmHg) or there is evidence of end-organ damage,
treatment should start without delay.

== 2. LABORATORY TESTING ==

WHO suggests obtaining baseline tests (serum electrolytes and creatinine, lipid
panel, HbA1c or fasting glucose, urine dipstick, ECG) to screen for comorbidities and
secondary hypertension — but only when testing would not delay or impede starting
treatment. In low-resource settings where testing isn't readily available, treatment
should not be delayed; testing can follow.

== 3. CARDIOVASCULAR RISK ASSESSMENT ==

Most patients with systolic >=140 or diastolic >=90 mmHg are already high-risk and
don't need formal CVD risk assessment before starting treatment. Risk assessment
matters most for the borderline 130-139 mmHg systolic group, to decide whether
earlier treatment is warranted, and should be done at or after treatment initiation
rather than as a gate that delays care.

== 4. FIRST-LINE DRUG CLASSES ==

WHO recommends starting with a drug from any of three classes (strong
recommendation, high-certainty evidence): thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics,
ACE inhibitors or ARBs (angiotensin-receptor blockers), or long-acting
dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers. Long-acting formulations are preferred.
Situational preferences: diuretics or CCBs for patients over 65 or of African descent;
beta-blockers for ischaemic heart disease; ACE inhibitors/ARBs for severe
proteinuria, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, or kidney disease.

== 5. COMBINATION THERAPY ==

WHO suggests starting combination therapy — preferably a single-pill combination
to improve adherence — chosen from the same three classes (diuretic, ACE
inhibitor/ARB, long-acting CCB), rather than titrating a single drug first. This is
especially valuable when baseline BP is 20/10 mmHg or more above target.
Single-pill combinations measurably improve medication adherence and persistence
compared to the same drugs taken as separate pills.

== 6. TARGET BLOOD PRESSURE ==

General target for patients with hypertension and no comorbidities: <140/90 mmHg
(strong recommendation). For patients with known cardiovascular disease: a lower
systolic target of <130 mmHg (strong recommendation). For high-risk patients without
known CVD but with high CVD risk, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease: a systolic
target of <130 mmHg is suggested (conditional recommendation) — the evidence for
this group is less certain than for those with established CVD.

== 7. FOLLOW-UP FREQUENCY ==

Monthly follow-up is suggested after starting or changing antihypertensive
medication, until the patient reaches target BP. Once BP is controlled, follow-up
every 3-6 months is suggested. Evidence indicates that waiting longer than roughly
6 weeks to start treatment, or longer than roughly 3 months to reassess after
starting, is associated with a meaningfully higher rate of major adverse
cardiovascular events.

== 8. NONPHYSICIAN-DELIVERED CARE ==

WHO suggests that pharmacists and nurses can prescribe and manage
antihypertensive treatment, provided they have proper training, prescribing
authority under local regulation, defined management protocols, and physician
oversight. Community health workers can assist with education, medication
delivery, and BP monitoring as part of a collaborative care model. Evidence shows
nonphysician-managed care achieves BP control at least as well as physician-only
care, with meaningfully better control rates in several studies (pharmacist-managed
programs in particular).
